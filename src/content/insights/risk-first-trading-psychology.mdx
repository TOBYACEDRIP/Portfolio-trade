Most traders don't fail because they don't understand order blocks. They fail because of what they do *after* a loss, *during* a winning streak, or when the market is quiet and they're bored. Psychology isn't separate from strategy — it's where strategy usually breaks.

## Discipline is a design problem

I don't think discipline is a personality trait you either have or don't. I think it's mostly about design. If your process requires perfect willpower at the worst possible moment, it will eventually fail.

So the goal is to make the right decision the *easy* decision:

- Risk is decided **before** the trade, not during it.
- Setups must meet **written criteria**, not a feeling.
- There are **hard limits** on daily and weekly losses that end the session automatically.

The fewer choices you leave for your emotional self, the fewer mistakes it can make.

## Risk is the first lesson, not the last

Every position should answer three questions before entry:

1. **Where is the idea wrong?** That's your invalidation and your stop.
2. **How much of my account am I willing to lose if it is?** That's your risk per trade.
3. **What size does that imply?** Position size is calculated from the first two, never the other way around.

Sizing from conviction — "this one looks really good" — is how one trade undoes a month of careful work.

## Losses are part of the model

No framework wins every trade, including mine. A process with a positive expectancy will still produce losing streaks, and you need to be able to survive them both financially and mentally.

> A loss that followed the plan is a good trade. A win that broke the plan is a bad one.

Judging trades by whether they followed the process — not by the outcome — is uncomfortable at first. It's also the only way to improve something you can actually control.

## The three moments that cost the most

**After a loss.** The urge to "make it back" leads to revenge trading: lower-quality setups, larger size, abandoned rules. A simple fix is a mandatory pause after a losing trade, and a hard stop after a set number of losses in a day.

**After a winning streak.** Overconfidence quietly loosens criteria and increases size. Keep risk constant; let consistency, not excitement, compound.

**When nothing is happening.** Boredom produces trades. Remember that no setup is a position — sitting out is part of the job.

## A note on prop firms

Prop firm challenges magnify all of this. Daily drawdown limits and maximum loss rules turn small emotional mistakes into failed accounts. The traders who handle them well usually:

- Risk **less** per trade than the rules allow, leaving room for normal losing streaks.
- Treat the drawdown limit as the edge of a cliff, not a target.
- Trade the same process they'd trade on their own capital — no special "challenge mode".

## Build a review habit

The most effective psychological tool I know is a journal you actually read. For every trade, record:

- The higher-timeframe context and bias.
- The point of interest and why it qualified.
- Whether the entry model was confirmed.
- How you felt before, during and after.
- Whether you followed the plan.

Patterns appear quickly. Most traders discover their losses cluster around a handful of repeat behaviours, and those are far easier to fix once they're written down.

## Final thought

Survival is the edge that makes every other edge possible. Protect the downside, keep the process simple enough to follow under pressure, and give yourself time. Consistency is built slowly — and it's built mostly by what you *don't* do.
